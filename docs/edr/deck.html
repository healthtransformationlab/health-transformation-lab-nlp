PerioBench: can finding gum disease accurately in the dental record improve care for millions of patients? Checked against gum specialists, built and tested end to end on synthetic records.

Synthetic patient records

Two open benchmarks: PerioBench, finding gum disease in dental records, built and tested on synthetic records; and OralHealthBench, AI answers on oral health and getting care, planned on top of GlobalDentBench.

John Bowyer · personal project, not affiliated with any organization

Gum disease is one of the most common chronic diseases in adults, and it is written down at almost every dental visit. But it is written down in pieces: a checkbox here, a billing code there, measurements, and a free-text note. That makes it hard to count the same way across clinics, and so hard to study at scale, link to diabetes, or see whether care is working. PerioBench asks one question: can finding gum disease accurately in the dental record improve care for people with it, so it is found earlier, treated, followed over time and managed alongside diabetes? Agreement with gum specialists reviewing the same records is how we check the result; better patient care is the goal. Everything shown is on synthetic records, built so the right answer is known; it is a rehearsal of the real study, not a result on real patients. This is a personal project, not any organization's plan.

Dental records rarely say plainly whether a patient has gum disease. The status has to be worked out from the evidence, the way a specialist would, before anyone can count it.

NHANES 2009–2014 (Eke et al., J Am Dent Assoc 2018) · EFP and IDF consensus report (Sanz et al., 2018) · seven evidence types: this repo · 2

Three facts. One: about 42 percent of US adults aged 30 and over who have teeth have periodontitis, gum disease that destroys the attachment and bone around teeth, and 7.8 percent have the severe form; that is the national survey, NHANES 2009 to 2014, published by Eke and colleagues in 2018. Two: gum disease and diabetes are linked in both directions; the European Federation of Periodontology and the International Diabetes Federation ask doctors and dentists to manage the two together. Three, and this is the one we can fix: in the dental record, whether a patient has gum disease is rarely written down once, reliably. It is spread over seven kinds of evidence: the clinician's note, an exam form with a score and a checkbox, the procedure codes done and planned, the diagnosis codes on those procedures, and the gum measurements. They often disagree. So before anyone can count gum disease across clinics, or link it to diabetes in the medical record, someone, or something, has to read the chart and work out the status. If asked for sources: Eke PI et al., Periodontitis in US adults: NHANES 2009-2014, JADA 2018, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2018.04.023; Sanz et al., J Clin Periodontol 2018, https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpe.12808. The seven evidence types are this study's design; see the evidence rules page.

PerioBench is three tests, and people often blur them, so here they are side by side. First, phenotyping: what stage of gum disease does this visit show? That is the study's answer. Rules read the whole record; AI reads the note alone. We compare BERT-family models trained on our notes with large language models used as they come, and the best AI gets 203 of 223 synthetic charts right. Second, de-identification: before anyone or any AI reads a real note, names, dates and record numbers have to go. Here the AI does a different job, labelling each word private or not, and the best combination leaves 115 of 2,714 identifiers in, while keeping the dental terms that look like names. Third, tooth loss: was a missing tooth lost to gum disease? There is no text here, so no BERT: logistic regression and XGBoost read the probing measurements and extraction codes, with an AUC of about 0.9 on held-out teeth. Why it matters: five or more teeth lost to gum disease is what makes stage 4. Every number is synthetic, scored against the generator's own answer key. Each question has its own leaderboard and its own pass bar, so cost is never compared across them: the next three slides put each one's cost beside its quality. The drill-down for each test ranks its models the same way, with paired tests and what drives the result.

The third question comes first in practice: before anyone or any AI reads a real note, names, dates and record numbers have to come out. We planted 2,714 fake identifiers in 400 synthetic notes, and count how many each method leaves in, with its 95 percent range, and how many dental words it removes by mistake, words like Glickman or Gracey that look like names. The ask

From synthetic to real

What we do

  • Run the same pipeline on a de-identified extract, inside its owner's environment and approvals
  • Four gum specialists label the validation charts, each on their own (~8 hours each)
  • Set the pass bars for tooth loss and de-identification before a real record is read
  • Publish each result against its bar, good or bad, with the code

If it works

  • Gum disease countable across clinics, in the format medicine uses
  • The medical-dental link, such as diabetes, studied at scale
  • A tested reader that checks AI answers against the record

The ask: a data partner willing to run it on a de-identified extract in its own environment, under its own approvals; four periodontists for about a day each; and a few days of GPU time. No partner has been contacted.

The ask is small, because the build is done. We need three things. One: a data partner willing to run the pipeline on a de-identified extract, under its own approvals; the code is built to run unchanged on a real extract, and nothing leaves their environment. Two: four gum specialists, about a day each, to label the validation charts independently; that is the 31 hours in the appendix's cost slide. Three: a few days of GPU time, for the larger models. And before any real record is read, the study lead sets the two pass bars that are still missing: the score a tooth-loss model must reach on teeth missing at the first visit, and the leak rate a de-identification method must stay under. In return: we publish each question's result against its bar, good or bad, with the code. If it works, gum disease becomes something you can count across clinics, in the same format as the medical record, so the link with diabetes can be studied at scale; and the same reader becomes a way to check AI answers against the record, which is the other path on the landing page. No partner has been contacted, and nothing here is any organization's plan. If asked what could go wrong: real notes are harder than synthetic ones, and the AI may not pass; that is still a publishable answer.

Appendix · What "passes" means

One question has a pass bar, not yet run. Two have none set.

Finding gum disease

Bar: agrees with four gum specialists at least as often as they agree with each other: mean Cohen's kappa, a margin of 0.05 fixed before looking (OHDSI case validation).

Status: not run. The reviewers so far are simulated, a dry run we don't show as a result.

Stand-in: could rank first on the synthetic answer key: the 95% range of its rank, over chart resamples shared by every method, reaches first place.

Tooth loss

Bar: none set. The analysis plan fixes the measures: AUC, Brier score, calibration, and sensitivity and specificity at 0.5 and at a threshold chosen on training teeth.

To decide: the AUC, or sensitivity at a fixed specificity, that passes, on teeth missing at the first visit, the ones the model is used for.

Stand-in: none; the slide shows the scores only.

De-identification

Bar: no leak rate set. On synthetic notes a final check holds back any note with a planted identifier left; on real notes only a second scan and a person checking 1 in 1,000 notes remain.

To decide: the leak rate a data owner accepts, and on which notes (the hardest tier leaks most).

Stand-in: could rank first on identifiers left in (95% Wilson ranges); dental words removed break ties.

Every score is synthetic, against the generator's own answer key: it shows the method works on records whose answer we know, not how it does on real records.

docs/process.md (validation criterion) · specs/029-tooth-loss/analysis_plan.md · docs/deidentification.md · rank ranges: docs/results.md · 9

What passes means is different for each question, and only one has a bar today. For finding gum disease, the bar is the acceptance test from OHDSI's case-validation work: a method passes if it agrees with four gum specialists at least as often as they agree with each other, measured with Cohen's kappa, with a margin of 0.05 fixed before anyone looks. It has not been run, because our reviewers are simulated so far, and a simulated result is a dry run we never show as a result. So the cost slide uses a stand-in, the top group: could this method rank first, over resamples of the same charts? For tooth loss, no bar is set: the analysis plan names the measures but not a passing score; we suggest setting it on the teeth missing at the first visit, because that is where the model is used and where it scores lowest. For de-identification, no leak rate is set either: on synthetic notes a final check holds back any note with a planted identifier left, but on real notes, where nothing is known in advance, only a second scan and a person checking one note in a thousand remain, so the leak rate needs a bar. Setting the two missing bars is part of the ask.

Before any software or person reads a note, names, dates and IDs come out. We compared 15 methods on 400 synthetic notes with fake identifiers planted in them. The best was a combination of tools with a guard that protects dental words; it still missed 115 of 2,714 identifiers, which is why a final check holds back any note with one left, and nothing goes on until it passes. The two numbers pull against each other, like a spam filter. We also tested on public benchmark sets, including published, already de-identified case reports. Synthetic charts, scored against the generator's truth · 2017 AAP/EFP classification · rules and worked examples: docs/evidence_rules.md · 14

A dental chart holds seven kinds of evidence: the clinician's note; the exam form, with a gum score and a status checkbox; the procedure codes done and planned; the diagnosis codes on those procedures, done and planned; and the probing chart of gum measurements, turned into a stage with the 2017 American Academy of Periodontology and European Federation of Periodontology worksheet. Procedure codes are only a rough guess from the treatment, so they are compared, not used. When the other sources disagree, the rules take the first one, in a fixed order, that gives a status. The order matters a lot. Reading the gum measurements first, the first rule set, gets 207 of 223 synthetic charts right; it is the 207 on the results slide. The study design's order, exam form first, then diagnosis codes, the note, and the measurements last, gets about 176, 79 percent. On synthetic charts the measurements are the generator's own evidence, which flatters reading them first; on real charts the answer may differ. Four sources can be ranked 24 ways; each ranking is scored against the specialists, and the one that agrees best is kept. A radiograph reading can only raise the stage.

How do we know an answer is right? On real records there is no answer key, so the acceptance test is the bar: an AI method passes only if it agrees with the specialists at least as often as they agree with each other, the standard from the OHDSI 2023 plenary on case validation, with the LLM review work published in npj Digital Medicine in 2025. We also tested whether a general-purpose AI, a large language model, could stand in for the answer key. Reading each note itself, it matched the answer key on 83 percent of charts but only 53 percent of hard notes, the ones that leave out what the answer key is built from. Asked whether a method's answer was right, it caught most mistakes of weak methods, but only 26 percent of the best note model's, which are near-misses such as one stage off. So it cannot replace specialists yet. These are AI judgments on synthetic notes, checked against the generator's truth, made on the review notes before they were rebuilt for adults; the judge is being rerun on the new notes. If asked about statistics: kappa for agreement beyond chance, 95 percent bootstrap intervals over charts, and unsafe errors counted separately.

This is the whole pipeline, and it runs end to end today on synthetic data. One: we generate synthetic patients across 8 clinics, visit after visit, with every kind of evidence and fake names in the notes, so the privacy step is tested too. Two: names, dates and IDs are removed, and a final check holds back any note that still has one. Three: the record goes into OMOP, the common data format medicine already uses, which is what lets a dental finding sit next to a diabetes record. Four: rules combine the whole record, and AI models read only the free-text note, for the many charts where the structured fields are missing. Five: gum specialists label the same 223 charts, independently; until real specialists do, simulated reviewers stand in so the reliability code can be tested, and their numbers are never shown as results. Six: the acceptance test. An AI method passes only if it agrees with the specialists at least as often as they agree with each other; that is the standard the OHDSI community uses to check how well records identify a disease. Because it is synthetic, we know the right answer for every chart, so we can test the test. If asked about the platform: Databricks and Unity Catalog, MLflow for models, Postgres and Lakebase for OMOP; Python and R give the same numbers.
