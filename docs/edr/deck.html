Two open benchmarks: PerioBench, finding gum disease in dental records, built and tested on synthetic records; and OralHealthBench, AI answers on oral health and getting care, planned on top of GlobalDentBench.

John Bowyer · personal project, not affiliated with any organization

Gum disease is one of the most common chronic diseases in adults, and it is written down at almost every dental visit. But it is written down in pieces: a checkbox here, a billing code there, measurements, and a free-text note. That makes it hard to count the same way across clinics, and so hard to study at scale, link to diabetes, or see whether care is working. PerioBench asks one question: can finding gum disease accurately in the dental record improve care for people with it, so it is found earlier, treated, followed over time and managed alongside diabetes? Agreement with gum specialists reviewing the same records is how we check the result; better patient care is the goal. Everything shown is on synthetic records, built so the right answer is known; it is a rehearsal of the real study, not a result on real patients. This is a personal project, not any organization's plan.

Dental records rarely say plainly whether a patient has gum disease. The status has to be worked out from the evidence, the way a specialist would, before anyone can count it.

NHANES 2009–2014 (Eke et al., J Am Dent Assoc 2018) · EFP and IDF consensus report (Sanz et al., 2018) · seven evidence types: this repo · 2

Three facts. One: about 42 percent of US adults aged 30 and over who have teeth have periodontitis, gum disease that destroys the attachment and bone around teeth, and 7.8 percent have the severe form; that is the national survey, NHANES 2009 to 2014, published by Eke and colleagues in 2018. Two: gum disease and diabetes are linked in both directions; the European Federation of Periodontology and the International Diabetes Federation ask doctors and dentists to manage the two together. Three, and this is the one we can fix: in the dental record, whether a patient has gum disease is rarely written down once, reliably. It is spread over seven kinds of evidence: the clinician's note, an exam form with a score and a checkbox, the procedure codes done and planned, the diagnosis codes on those procedures, and the gum measurements. They often disagree. So before anyone can count gum disease across clinics, or link it to diabetes in the medical record, someone, or something, has to read the chart and work out the status. If asked for sources: Eke PI et al., Periodontitis in US adults: NHANES 2009-2014, JADA 2018, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adaj.2018.04.023; Sanz et al., J Clin Periodontol 2018, https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpe.12808. The seven evidence types are this study's design; see the evidence rules page.

Synthetic patient records · Health Transformation Lab · the dental record (EDR) · 3

This is the whole pipeline, and it runs end to end today on synthetic data. One: we generate synthetic patients across 8 clinics, visit after visit, with every kind of evidence and fake names in the notes, so the privacy step is tested too. Two: names, dates and IDs are removed, and a final check holds back any note that still has one. Three: the record goes into OMOP, the common data format medicine already uses, which is what lets a dental finding sit next to a diabetes record. Four: rules combine the whole record, and AI models read only the free-text note, for the many charts where the structured fields are missing. Five: gum specialists label the same 240 charts, independently; until real specialists do, simulated reviewers stand in so the reliability code can be tested, and their numbers are never shown as results. Six: the acceptance test. An AI method passes only if it agrees with the specialists at least as often as they agree with each other; that is the standard the OHDSI community uses to check how well records identify a disease. Because it is synthetic, we know the right answer for every chart, so we can test the test. If asked about the platform: Databricks and Unity Catalog, MLflow for models, Postgres and Lakebase for OMOP; Python and R give the same numbers.

These are real model runs, on synthetic charts, scored against the generator's truth; they are not clinical performance, and agreement with simulated reviewers is a dry run we do not show. Four numbers out of 221 review charts: the 240 review charts less the 19 visits before April 2021, the start date the scores use, with healthy and gingivitis counted as one group. Rules that combine the whole record, reading the gum measurements first, got 215 right. Reading only the free-text note, a small model trained on dental notes, ModernBERT with a keyword rule for the most severe stage, got 201. A bigger medical AI used as is, without training and with the same stage 4 rule: MedGemma 4B got 181, and the newer MedGemma 1.5 4B got 177. Both are close to simple keyword rules at 179; on 221 charts neither can be told apart from them. Alone they each get about 174. And any single piece of evidence falls short: the gum measurements alone got 177 right, because 42 of the 221 charts had no measurements at all. Two lessons: combine the evidence, and train on the domain. If asked about uncertainty: every number has a 95 percent bootstrap interval on the full results page; on 221 charts, many methods cannot be told apart. If asked whether MedGemma can improve: few-shot and fine-tuned runs are next, on a GPU. If asked why synthetic: no identifiable patient data may be used here; synthetic data lets us build and test everything, including the privacy checks, before any approvals.

The economics, and it depends on the model. Reading a million visits by hand, at the protocol's pace of about 2 minutes a chart, is 33,333 hours of a specialist's time. Software for the same million visits ranges from under 15 dollars to about 1.2 million, depending on the model. A small model trained on dental notes, on our own hardware, costs under 15 dollars, at illustrative compute prices. An open model hosted per token, Llama 3.3 70B at Databricks list price, costs about 260 dollars for the note alone. A frontier commercial model at list price costs about 1,100 dollars for the note alone with Google's Gemini 3.1 Pro, and about 5,500 with Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1. And if a frontier model reads the whole record, with worked examples and reasoning, three calls a visit, it is about 260 thousand dollars with Gemini and about 1.2 million with Fable; batch pricing halves either. Those token counts are assumed, about 20 thousand in and 4 thousand out a call, not measured. The note-only token count is estimated from our synthetic notes, characters divided by four, and they are shorter than real ones; the prompt and answer lengths are assumed. The acceptance test is small: about 31 specialist hours, 233 charts each read by 4 reviewers. So the choice is the least costly model that passes the acceptance test. If asked for a dollar figure for people: at an illustrative 100 dollars an hour, validation is about 3,100 dollars and reviewing everything by hand about 3.3 million. If asked about errors: the study also counts unsafe errors, such as missing severe disease, and reports them separately.

The ask is small, because the build is done. We need three things. Orders to test

24

So the order is a finding, not a setting: the ranking that agrees best with the specialists is the one to keep. Procedure codes are a rough guess from the treatment, so they are compared, not used.

Synthetic charts, scored against the generator's truth · 2017 AAP/EFP classification · rules and worked examples: docs/evidence_rules.md · 8

A dental chart holds seven kinds of evidence: the clinician's note; the exam form, with a gum score and a status checkbox; the procedure codes done and planned; the diagnosis codes on those procedures, done and planned; and the probing chart of gum measurements, turned into a stage with the 2017 American Academy of Periodontology and European Federation of Periodontology worksheet. Procedure codes are only a rough guess from the treatment, so they are compared, not used. When the other sources disagree, the rules take the first one, in a fixed order, that gives a status. The order matters a lot. Reading the gum measurements first, the first rule set, gets 215 of 221 synthetic charts right; it is the 215 on the results slide. The study design's order, exam form first, then diagnosis codes, the note, and the measurements last, gets about 163, 74 percent. On synthetic charts the measurements are the generator's own evidence, which flatters reading them first; on real charts the answer may differ. Four sources can be ranked 24 ways; each ranking is scored against the specialists, and the one that agrees best is kept. A radiograph reading can only raise the stage.

Synthetic charts; LLM judgments checked against the generator's truth · the bar: OHDSI 2023 plenary on case validation; npj Digit Med 2025 · 9

How do we know an answer is right? On real records there is no answer key, so the acceptance test is the bar: an AI method passes only if it agrees with the specialists at least as often as they agree with each other, the standard from the OHDSI 2023 plenary on case validation, with the LLM review work published in npj Digital Medicine in 2025. We also tested whether a general-purpose AI, a large language model, could stand in for the answer key. Reading each note itself, it matched the answer key on 83 percent of charts but only 53 percent of hard notes, the ones that leave out what the answer key is built from. Asked whether a method's answer was right, it caught most mistakes of weak methods, but only 26 percent of the best note model's, which are near-misses such as one stage off. So it cannot replace specialists yet. These are AI judgments on synthetic notes, checked against the generator's truth. If asked about statistics: kappa for agreement beyond chance, 95 percent bootstrap intervals over charts, and unsafe errors counted separately.

Methods compared

Two numbers that pull against each other: remove too little and patients are at risk; remove too much and the note the AI reads is damaged.

Synthetic notes with fake names and dates; also tested on public benchmark sets · Health Transformation Lab · the dental record (EDR) · 10

Before any software or person reads a note, names, dates and IDs come out. We compared 15 methods on 400 synthetic notes with fake identifiers planted in them. The best was a combination of tools with a guard that protects dental words; it still missed 115 of 2,714 identifiers, which is why a final check holds back any note with one left, and nothing goes on until it passes. It also removed 41 of 2,462 dental terms that look like names or codes, such as Glickman or Gracey 11/12, which would damage what the AI reads. The two numbers pull against each other, like a spam filter. We also tested on public benchmark sets, including published, already de-identified case reports. All synthetic, or published by others already de-identified.
