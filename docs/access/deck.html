Millions ask AI how to get care.

Does the answer get them there?

AccessBench grades whether an AI answer helps someone actually get care: is it covered, what to do about a denial or a bill, where to go that they can afford.

People now ask AI the questions they used to bring to a benefits counselor or a dental office's front desk: is this covered, what do I do about this bill, where can I go that I can afford. AccessBench grades whether the answer actually gets them to care. This is a personal project, not any organization's plan, and every question is synthetic, written from public sources.

People are not waiting for AI to be approved for health questions. OpenAI reported in January 2026 that more than 40 million people a day ask ChatGPT about health, worldwide, and that it gets between 1.6 and 1.9 million health-insurance messages a week. That is OpenAI's own figure, as reported by Axios and Medical Economics, not an independent count. And a 50-state survey of more than 20,000 US adults in spring 2025, published in JAMA Network Open in 2026, found 10.3 percent use generative AI at least daily; that survey drew from an internet panel, not a probability sample. The point is the kind of question: coverage, bills, appeals and where to go. Those used to go to a benefits counselor, a front desk or a legal-aid line.

Accuracy studies of chatbots on dental questions find real gaps. A 2026 meta-analysis in Dental Traumatology pooled 10 studies of advice on dental injuries: accuracy was 65 percent for ChatGPT 3.5, 79 percent for ChatGPT 4 and 70 percent for Gemini, so roughly one answer in five to one in three was inaccurate, and the authors warn that even limited inaccuracies can delay care. GlobalDentBench, a 2026 preprint, found that 1 in 3 of those answers was rated unsafe by the study's AI judge on case questions from real dental reports, across 12 frontier models. Getting care is harder than clinical facts, because the right answer depends on the state, the plan, the person's age and a deadline. Adult Medicaid dental is optional for states, and Medicare excludes routine dental care. One fact can flip the answer: a 19-year-old on Medicaid is covered by the under-21 rule called EPSDT; a 23-year-old is not. Nobody was measuring whether AI gets these right. That is the gap AccessBench fills.

How AccessBench addresses it

After reading the answer, could you take the next step?

Real situations

Written from public questions

Medicaid coverage and denials, Medicare's dental exclusion, waiting periods, discount plans and medical credit cards, rides, VA dental, wheelchair access, a child on CHIP. The people are invented; the rules are real.

Graded for action

Not for sounding right

Did it make the points this person needs? Give a usable next step: what to do first, whom to call, when, and a fallback? Avoid the confident wrong turn, such as "a denial means you owe"?

Graded for safety

Harm and privacy apart

Any harmful advice is graded, never offset by points earned elsewhere. Answers must not repeat a person's name, phone or ID back.

Every answer key was reviewed by five other AI models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity, Grok) and revised; seeded control answers check that the AI judge can tell a harmful answer from a good one.

AccessBench asks one question of every answer: after reading it, could this person take the next step? The questions are written from real public questions, on Medicaid coverage and denials, Medicare's dental exclusion, waiting periods, discount plans and medical credit cards, rides, VA dental, wheelchair access and a child on CHIP. The people are invented; the rules are real. Each answer is graded for action, not for sounding right: did it make the points this person needs, did it give a usable next step, did it avoid the confident wrong turn. Harm and privacy are graded separately, so a harmful answer can't make up for it with points elsewhere. Five other AI models reviewed the questions and answer keys, and the keys were revised from their reviews. And seeded control answers, written to known grades, check that the AI judge can tell harmful advice from good advice.

This is the first run on the revised answer keys, on four open models hosted on Databricks. Across all four, 187 of 352 key points were made, about half. gpt-oss-120b made 77.7 percent of the points and Gemma 3 12B 72.7 percent; the two Llama models made about a third, 32.5 and 29.1 percent. Most answers did offer a next step of some kind. The judge graded some harm in 15 answers and serious harm in one, Gemma's answer on a painful tooth. Two cautions. gpt-oss-120b ran out of its answer budget on 18 of 22 questions, so part of its score may come from listing everything. And these grades come from one AI judge that has not yet been checked against an independent human reviewer, so they are preliminary. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are not in this run yet.

What every model missed

Pain and swelling

0 of 4 said: get urgent care now

A person in pain, waiting on a gum-treatment decision, and a parent with a child's broken tooth: no model said swelling or fever needs care today.

0 of 4 caught it at 23

At 19, 2 of 4 models named the under-21 Medicaid rule. At 23, when it no longer applies, none said so, and one said it still did.

The programs people don't know

The VA's dental insurance program for veterans without VA dental; the difference between Medicaid and a separate CHIP program for a child.

These are not trivia. Each missing step is the difference between getting care this week and an emergency visit, a cash bill or giving up.

Where the models fell short is the point of AccessBench. Two questions involved pain: a person whose office won't treat a painful wisdom tooth until gum treatment is approved, and a parent whose child has a broken tooth. No model said that swelling, fever or trouble swallowing needs urgent care today. The Florida pair shows how one fact changes the answer: at 19, two of the four models named the under-21 Medicaid rule, EPSDT, that covers medically necessary dental care; at 23, when it no longer applies, none of the four said so, and one said it still did. And no model named the VA Dental Insurance Program for a veteran without VA dental, or told a parent to find out whether their child is in Medicaid or a separate CHIP program, which decides the dental rules. Each missing step is the difference between getting care this week and an emergency visit, a cash bill or giving up. The judge itself passed 6 of 10 seeded control answers: it caught both clearly harmful answers and credited both good ones, but it counts incomplete answers as some harm, which still needs a decision.

Appendix: how it works

Questions, grading and the run

Questions

22 in three groups

Gum-care coverage and appeals, dental discounts and vouchers, and access questions with invented personal details. Each names its jurisdiction, its sources and a date to recheck them.

Grading

An AI judge, point by point

For each point: met, partial, missing or contradicted, with the words that earned it. Plus next step, harm (none, some, serious), resources by fit, and the privacy check.

The run

One Databricks job

Each model answers each question; the judge grades; results land in two gold tables with cost and tokens. A spending cap stops a run before it overspends.

Every person in every question is invented; real patient data never enters AccessBench. Results are preliminary until an independent reviewer has graded answers.

For those who want the method. There are 22 questions in three groups: getting gum care covered and appealing denials, dental discounts and vouchers, and access questions that include invented personal details to test privacy. Each question names its jurisdiction, its sources and a date to recheck them, because these rules change. An AI judge grades each answer point by point: met, partial, missing or contradicted, quoting the words that earned it; it also checks for a usable next step, grades harm as none, some or serious, rates resources by how well they fit, and the privacy check looks for repeated identifiers. One Databricks job sends every question to every model, has the judge grade the answers, and writes the results with cost and tokens to two gold tables; a spending cap stops a run before it overspends. Every person is invented, and results stay preliminary until an independent reviewer has graded answers.
