AccessBench asks whether an AI answer helps someone actually get care: coverage, cost, appeals, travel and accommodations, not only whether the facts are sound.

AccessBench is the third of the three benchmarks, and the most exploratory. PerioBench asks whether we can find gum disease in the dental record; OralHealthBench asks whether AI gives accurate answers where medicine meets dentistry. AccessBench asks the question people actually live with: after reading the answer, can I get the care? An answer can be medically correct and still useless if it ignores that the person is on Medicaid in one particular state, has a denial letter in hand, or has a one-year waiting period. This is a personal project, not any organization's plan, and every question is synthetic, written from public sources.

The gap

Getting care is a pathway, not a fact

Coverage

Is it covered on my plan, in my state?

Adult Medicaid dental is optional for states, so the right answer depends on the state and the plan (CMS).

Denials

They said no. What now?

A written denial, the appeal deadline, the dentist's charting and narrative: the steps decide whether care happens.

The visit itself

Did I get what I needed?

A cleaning is not a periodontal evaluation with probing and charting (ADA/IHS coding guidance for D0180).

Most health AI benchmarks ask whether the facts are right. For getting care, the facts are only the start. Three things decide whether someone gets a gum check. Coverage: adult dental benefits are optional for state Medicaid programs, so a good answer says it depends on the state and the plan, and tells the person how to check. Denials: when a plan says no, what matters is getting the denial in writing, the appeal deadline, and the dentist's charting and narrative. And the visit itself: a polish is not a periodontal evaluation; the coding guidance separates the comprehensive periodontal evaluation, code D0180, from a routine cleaning. AccessBench scores whether the answer names the next concrete step for this person, and whether it avoids the confident wrong turn.

The first questions

Ten hard asks about getting a gum check

  • Medicaid coverage by state: New York, Florida (under-21), Medi-Cal, pregnancy coverage
  • Denials and appeals: a deep cleaning denied for "no bone loss", prior authorization refused, x-rays rejected
  • Private plans: a one-year waiting period for periodontics
  • Medicare and medical insurance: the dental exclusion and its narrow exceptions (42 CFR 411.15(i)); diabetes does not move care onto medical benefits
  • The visit: a cleaning versus a comprehensive periodontal evaluation (D0180), and what to ask the office to chart

Each ask is written from a real public question (forum threads, KFF, IHS coding guidance) and scored against published sources only. The questions are synthetic: no one's own words or details.

How an answer is scored

Must say, must not say

Must include

4 key points per question

The steps that get this person care, such as: get the denial in writing, ask which procedure codes were billed, call the state's dental line.

Red flags

1 to 2 per question

The confident wrong turn, such as "all adult Medicaid covers gum exams" or "ignore the bill". Any red flag the answer says is recorded, with a harm grade (S0 to S2).

The judge

Key points, one by one

A second model checks each point, five per question as in GlobalDentBench (red flags fill any gap). Harder, date-sensitive questions are level L3.

The score is the share of key points covered; the access gate passes only when the key points are met and no red flag is said. Each question names its evidence source, so a reviewer can check the rubric itself.

Each question carries its own rubric. Four things a good answer must include: the concrete steps that lead to care for this person. And one or two red flags: the confident wrong turns, such as claiming every adult Medicaid program covers gum exams, or telling someone to ignore a bill. A red flag is recorded with a harm grade, and the access gate fails it even if the key points are there, because a wrong pathway can cost someone the visit or a large bill. A second model acts as judge and checks the key points one at a time, using the key-point grader of the grader dashboards (spec 019), five key points per question as in GlobalDentBench, the red flags filling any gap; the score is the share of key points covered. Each question names its evidence, so the rubric itself can be reviewed by someone who knows the programs.

People asking for help often tell the AI who they are: a name, a phone number, a member ID. AccessBench has a second small set of five questions that do exactly that, across five kinds of access problem: no insurance, no car, VA care, a wheelchair, and a child on CHIP. Every planted detail is invented; the Social Security numbers start with 9, which are never issued. Real patient data is never allowed in AccessBench.

Built to run

One job scores every model the same way

  • Databricks job "Access LLM batch" sends each question to each answering model, then to the judge
  • Two gold tables: dim_access_question (one row per question) and fact_access_answer (one row per answer, with its verdicts, tokens and cost)
  • Simulated by default: no model is called and nothing is billed; simulated rows are labelled simulated
  • Live mode uses pay-per-token endpoints and stops when its spending limit is reached

No live scores yet. The job, the tables and the rubrics are built and tested; the first live run waits for the owner's go-ahead on which models and what budget.

The plumbing is built. A Databricks job called Access LLM batch sends each question to each answering model and then to the judge, and writes the results into two gold tables: one row per question, and one row per answer with its verdicts, tokens and cost. By default it runs in simulated mode: no model is called, nothing is billed, and the rows say simulated. Live mode uses pay-per-token endpoints and stops when it reaches its spending limit. There are no live scores yet, and this deck shows none. The first live run waits for a decision on which models to compare and how much to spend.
