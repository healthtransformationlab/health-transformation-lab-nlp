Health Transformation Lab

A personal idea

Two open benchmarks: PerioBench, finding gum disease in dental records, built and tested on synthetic records; and OralHealthBench, AI answers on oral health and getting care, planned on top of GlobalDentBench.

John Bowyer · personal project, not affiliated with any organization

AI passes the dental exam, and it fails the real patient. Most AI benchmarks test exam knowledge, and published studies of real patient cases have found AI falling short. On real hospital cases, AI models didn't follow the clinical guidelines doctors use (Hager and colleagues, Nature Medicine, 2024). Members of the public using AI did no better than those using a source of their choice (Bean and colleagues, Nature Medicine, 2026). And on drug interactions, chatbots were often wrong where a standard interaction checker was right (Al-Ashwal and colleagues, 2023). I am proposing an independent, open lab, called the Health Transformation Lab. Its mission: better oral health for millions of Americans, and better overall health through the links between medical and dental care. It does that by making AI accurate on two things today's tests leave out. One is where medicine meets dentistry: medicines, chronic disease such as diabetes, and emergencies. The other is the social factors that decide whether people get care: cost, coverage, distance and transportation, what researchers call the social determinants of health. This is a personal idea: nothing has been registered or built, no organization is behind it, and no partner has agreed to anything.

AI is graded on public scorecards (called benchmarks), and AI companies compete on the scores. The first one for dentistry came out this year, so the questions are being written now.

Averages for 12 AI models, from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, xAI and others · GlobalDentBench 2026; first search Oct 2026 · 2

First, how AI gets graded. Independent researchers publish tests and score every major AI on them, like a public report card. The technical word is benchmark. AI companies compete to raise those scores, so what is on the scorecard is what gets better. Now four facts from the first dental scorecard. One: AI scores 81 percent on multiple-choice questions from dental licensing exams. Two: on questions built from published patient case reports, the score falls to 22 percent. Three: nearly one in three of those answers was rated unsafe, and about one in twenty risked harm that cannot be undone. Four, and this is the one we can change: we found nothing that tests the social side. No scorecard we found checks whether AI answers accurately on the social factors that decide whether people get care: cost, coverage, distance and transportation. US emergency rooms see about 1.9 million visits a year for tooth problems, and Medicaid is the most common payer for them (CDC, NCHS Data Brief 531, 2025); for people without a dentist, the untested questions are the ones that matter most. Why now: the first global dental scorecard for AI was published this year, so the questions are being written right now, and the people who show up shape them. If asked for detail: the first three are from GlobalDentBench, published in 2026: 12 leading AI models, from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, xAI and others, 8,978 questions from 88 countries; the scores are averages across all 12, not one company's product, and Google's model scored highest overall; 31 percent of case answers were unsafe and 4.5 percent carried a risk of irreversible harm. The fourth is from a first search of benchmarks, not a full review: existing tests check whether AI can spot social factors in medical records, or whether answers are biased, but none we found checks accuracy on access to care. If asked whether anyone has studied AI in dentistry: yes. A systematic review of 38 studies (Kaplan and Akyol, Japanese Dental Science Review, 2026; literature searched January 2025) found no universally accepted framework for evaluating AI in dentistry, test methods and sizes that vary widely, mostly simulated scenarios rather than real-world use, and limited evidence on how patients interpret and act on AI answers. Its categories are patient questions about treatment, diagnosis, model comparisons and decision support; none is access to care. The authors also say continuous monitoring is essential because new models keep appearing. The example illustrates a harm GlobalDentBench (2026) names. The 3 in 4 is from NOHARM (Stanford-led ARISE network, 2026) · 3

Here is what unsafe means. The study found AI is most dangerous in two areas. Where medicines and medical conditions are involved, about one in seven answers risked harm that cannot be undone. On anesthesia and medical emergencies, about one in eleven. The simplest example: someone on a bone-strengthening medicine for osteoporosis asks if it is fine to have a tooth pulled. The unsafe answer says yes, it is routine, and never asks about the medicine. In some patients on these medicines the jawbone does not heal after an extraction, and that is permanent. If asked for detail: the figures are 14.15 percent for the category the study calls Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety, and 8.93 percent for Anesthesia and Medical Emergencies, averaged across 12 AI models. The study lists this jaw condition, medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, among its examples of irreversible harm, along with avoidable tooth loss, permanent nerve injury and delayed treatment of cancer or severe infection. The question and answer on this slide are my illustration of that harm: the study publishes the rates, not the individual wrong answers. The risk to any one patient is low at osteoporosis doses and higher at cancer-treatment doses, which is why the dentist needs to know about the medicine. The line at the bottom is from a different study, called First, Do NOHARM, run by the Stanford-led ARISE network on 1,100 real medical cases: 76.6 percent of the harmful errors were omissions, things the AI left out. That is the same pattern as this example, and it is also how AI fails on social factors: an answer that never asks about cost, coverage or transportation leaves out what decides whether the person gets care. NOHARM is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed, and it has no oral health cases. If asked for an emergency example: a person with a swollen face and a fever from a toothache is told to rinse with salt water and wait; a spreading infection can become life-threatening.

CDC NCHS Data Brief 531 (2025; visits 2020–2022) · EFP and IDF consensus report on periodontitis and diabetes (Sanz et al., 2018) · GlobalDentBench 2026 · 4

This is where the biggest health gain is: the seam between medical and dental care. Three facts. One: US emergency rooms see about 1.9 million visits a year for tooth problems, on average over 2020 to 2022, and Medicaid is the most common payer; an emergency room can relieve pain and infection but rarely treats the tooth, so many people come back. Two: diabetes and gum disease are linked in both directions. A joint consensus report from the European Federation of Periodontology and the International Diabetes Federation asks doctors and dentists to screen for and manage the two together. Three: medicines change dental care; a bone-strengthening medicine can make an extraction unsafe, which is the harm on the previous slide. In each case the doctor holds half the picture and the dentist holds the other half, and both now ask AI. Studies of real patient cases have found AI falling short on questions like these, drug interactions among them, so that is where the questions go first, alongside the social factors that decide whether the person gets care at all. If asked for sources: CDC, NCHS Data Brief 531, Emergency Department Visits for Tooth Disorders: United States, 2020-2022 (June 2025), https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db531.htm; Sanz et al., Scientific evidence on the links between periodontal diseases and diabetes, EFP and IDF consensus report, Journal of Clinical Periodontology 2018, https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpe.12808. The visit count is a national survey estimate, and the diabetes link is an association described by the consensus, not a promise that treating one cures the other.

Today, oral health research reaches thousands of experts, indirectly: they find the papers, reports and data portals, and each study measures its own impact. That work stays. What OralHealthBench adds reaches millions, directly, in public, with third parties verifying it. The main move is this: put the medical-dental link and the social factors of care on the public scorecards that grade AI. Every question traces back to published evidence, so anyone can drill down from the test to the science behind it. Once the questions are on the scorecard, every AI model tested on that scorecard is graded on them, and AI companies work to improve what they are graded on. An open AI agent is the second step: it shows how much better the answers get with the evidence. If asked about the numbers: thousands is my description of the research audience, not a measured figure. Millions is the audience of the public AI tools: OpenAI reported in January 2026 that more than 40 million people a day ask its AI assistant health questions, worldwide, including 1.6 to 1.9 million health insurance questions a week; Google reported 950 million monthly users of its AI assistant in July 2026. That is the reach available, not a promise. We found no published count of how many of those questions are dental; measuring that could be an early result of the 180 days. If asked whether the people who run the scorecards will accept the questions: it is their decision, so we ask as partners.

We measure in public: accuracy, safety, and the agent’s share of the gain. The goal is to shape the benchmarks, not only to score well · Drill-down

Here is how it works, in one question. Someone asks an oral health question: a doctor, a patient, a policymaker. They ask it in the AI tool they already use. Today that AI answers from whatever it already knows. With the open agent connected, the AI first checks published research, public guidelines and public data, and answers from that, showing the source. Over time the answer carries three things: the human story, the number behind it, and the source. We do not replace their AI. We sit behind it. The uplift is how much better the answer gets with the agent than without it. And because the connection is published, any organization can plug it into the AI it already uses. The bigger goal is not just a high score. It is to shape the tests themselves, because what gets tested is what AI developers work to improve. So there is a second way to help: work with the people who run the dental test to add the questions that are missing, on the medical-dental link and on access to care and its barriers. Where we expect the most gain, to be straight about it: public evidence is strongest on access, cost, coverage and the link between oral and overall health, and the dental test does not cover those areas yet. Exact terms if a technical person asks: the AI is the model; the published connection is an MCP server; the model using that connection is the agent.

Someone may ask whether public AI tests exist beyond clinical dentistry. From a first search: partly, and nothing specific to oral health. For medical-dental integration, the closest is one category of the global dental test, covering systemic health, medicines and safety. For the social side of health, often called social determinants or SDOH, the existing tests mostly check whether AI can spot social factors, such as housing or transport, inside medical records. Google has also published health equity question sets that check for biased answers. I found none that tests whether an AI answer actually works for a person given their circumstances, such as no coverage, no car, or no dentist nearby, and none for oral health. That is the test most worth writing, and it is the same ground as the access-to-care questions. For education, there are many tests built from licensing exams, but they measure what the AI knows, not whether a professional learned anything. For grants, I found one 2026 study that had AI review six UK research grant proposals; it is a method, not a standing test, and nothing covers philanthropic grantmaking. For policy and advocacy, there is a UK public health guidance test called PubHealthBench, with over eight thousand questions, and a government policy test called POLIS-Bench; nothing covers oral health policy. So the gap is the opening: where no test exists, OralHealthBench can help write one with partners. This was a first search, not a full review, so say that if pressed.

It reads medical images, and Google’s list does not include dental. A dental version would be new, and open to share.

Public access through Hugging Face and MCP

Three paths in, each with safeguards

Path 1

Open

Anyone. Benchmarks, definitions, model cards and aggregate, de-identified measures under an open license.

Path 2

Registered

Sign in and accept terms of use. The models and agents behind PerioBench and OralHealthBench, called through MCP or an API, with usage limits and logging.

Path 3

Agreement

A signed data use agreement, reviewed and approved, for detailed or licensed data in a secure environment.

Safeguards on all three paths: no personal health information leaves the platform; licensed and payer data stay under the agreements they came with; each model and agent is benchmarked and reviewed by privacy and legal before release.

Proposed design, for privacy and legal review · Drill-down

Hugging Face is the hub where AI builders find models and data. MCP is the open standard that lets any AI assistant call outside tools. Opening up does not mean giving everything away. Three paths: open for benchmarks and aggregate material, registered for the agents, and a data use agreement for anything detailed. This is a proposed design; privacy and legal review decide the final rules, and each dataset's license limits what can be shared.

Building on the standards

Embrace the standards, then extend them

Embrace

Map oral health data to the standard medicine already uses, so medical researchers can work with it on day one.

Extend

Help lead OMOP's dental extension, and work with standards bodies to get oral health into the standard healthcare benchmarks.

Connect

Knowing how health data connects in OMOP also builds the graph and search layers that give AI its knowledge.

These are shared standards. Oral health can hold a pivotal seat at the table.

Health Transformation Lab · Drill-down

OMOP is the common data format for health research. Dentistry is the open seat: its dentistry workgroup began in 2022 and reports few dental datasets converted so far. The same goes for AI benchmarks: work with the bodies that own them to add oral health. If dental data speaks that format, medicine comes to oral health without translation, and that is how oral health becomes part of overall health in practice. The knowledge carries over: OMOP is a map of how health terms relate, such as which code is a kind of which condition. The same map is what a knowledge graph and an AI search index are built from, so the skill that maps dental data to OMOP is the skill that makes frontier models more knowledgeable about oral health. It has been done

Niche tools that became infrastructure

REDCap

Started: 2004, a data tool for Vanderbilt's own researchers.

Became: free to nonprofits; over 8,500 institutions in 167 countries.

OHDSI

Started: a narrow drug-safety research project.

Became: a global open-science community: the OMOP data standard, shared vocabularies, open-source tools and network studies.

Healthtalk.org

Started: 2001, two Oxford doctors collecting patients' own stories.

Became: research-grade story library; its method adopted in 14 countries.

The pattern: own the shared standard, give the tool away, let others bring the content.

Sources: Vanderbilt CTTC; OHDSI; healthtalk.org · Drill-down

Pick one and tell it in two sentences. REDCap is the closest match: built for internal researchers, given away, and now the center of its field. Each of these started small, with an expert group, as this lab would.

The plan in phases

A phased start, sized for part-time work

Phase 1

First 180 days. Build one open AI agent on published research, public guidelines and synthetic records with known answers. Test it on medical-dental and access-to-care questions, with and without it, and measure the uplift.

Phase 2

If phase 1 earns it. Tune an open medical AI model, Google’s MedGemma, on de-identified oral health data. That gives oral health an open model anyone can check.

Phase 3

Later. Publish it after privacy and legal review, open it to partners with safeguards, and report whether it helped people.

The ask covers phase 1 only: a small team at 5 to 10 hours a week each for 180 days, $50,000, and a data-platform partner to approach: Databricks for Good, a candidate, not yet contacted.

Health Transformation Lab · Drill-down

Each phase starts only if the one before it earned it. Phase 1 is deliberately small because it is part-time work alongside other priorities. The dates for phases 2 and 3 are set after phase 1 reports.
