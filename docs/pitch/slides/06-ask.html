If it works

  • AI graded on the medical-dental link and the social factors of care, not only clinical knowledge
  • Public evidence as the source behind every question
  • The major AI models held publicly accountable for accuracy, for the size of a small marketing budget

The ask: a small team, 5 to 10 hours a week each, for 180 days; $50,000; and partners: the owners of the public dental benchmarks, the Stanford-led ARISE network, which ranks AI on patient harm; and, for the data platform, Databricks for Good as a candidate.

Databricks for Good: databricks.com, Data + AI Summit session · Health Transformation Lab · 6

The ask has three parts. One: a small team, each giving 5 to 10 hours a week for 180 days. Two: 50 thousand dollars. That is the size of a small marketing budget, and it buys something marketing cannot: a public, accurate way to hold the major AI models accountable on oral health. Three: partners. The people who run the public dental benchmarks, because the questions belong on their scorecards. And the ARISE network, led from Stanford, which ranks AI on patient harm and has no oral health cases; we would offer them ours. For the data platform, the candidate is Databricks for Good, which, in Databricks' own words, helps mission-driven organizations through nonprofit pricing, technical enablement and hands-on support from Databricks experts. It has not been contacted, and whether a personal project qualifies has not been checked. If asked why ARISE would work with us: their cases come from primary care and emergency rooms, which is where a patient with a toothache and no dentist ends up, and they have found that most harmful errors are things AI leaves out. We bring oral health cases, the medical-dental questions and the social-factors questions they lack. Nobody has been contacted yet, no partner has agreed to anything, and it is their decision. If asked what the 50 thousand dollars pays for: AI usage for the testing, clinician time to write and check the questions, and publishing the results; a line-by-line budget is not set yet. The team needs at least one clinician or scientist, to write and judge the questions, and one engineer, to build the agent and the software that runs the tests. In that time we do three things. We write the missing scorecard questions on the medical-dental link and the social factors of care, each linked to the published evidence behind it. We test the major AI models on them, with and without an open agent, and publish the results. And with partners, we take those results to the AI companies and the people who run the scorecards, and ask for change. Publishing is what creates the pressure: a public result is harder for the AI companies to ignore. We found no measurement of this, so the gain could be large or small. 180 days tells us. If asked what the questions cover: what a doctor, a dentist or a person with no dentist asks where medical meets dental, and about getting care, such as when to go, where to go and how to pay, judged on whether the answer works given their circumstances. If asked what the agent is built on: published research, public clinical guidelines and public data, plus synthetic patient records whose right answers are known. If asked about safety: the agent is built to point people toward care, not to replace a dentist or doctor, and an answer that would delay urgent care is scored as unsafe. If asked about sample size: about 25 questions per round is a pilot, enough to see direction. If asked why 180 days: at 5 to 10 hours a week each, the work needs the time, and nobody is pulled off their main job. If asked how this holds anyone accountable: the scores are public and checked by third parties, so when a major AI model answers poorly, anyone can see it, and the companies have a reason to fix it. That depends on the scorecard owners accepting the questions; if they do not, we can still publish our own results. If asked what happens if there is no uplift: we learned it at small cost, and the questions are still a contribution. If asked about the moonshot: AI that works for everyone on oral health and connects it to the rest of their health, reached one public, measured step at a time.
