Before the problem, the three answers people ask for first: who this is for, what we want them to do, and what changes. The audience that has to act is the AI companies, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and the others, because their AI is where millions of people now ask health questions. We ask them to test their models on these questions and fix the answers that fail, the unsafe ones first. In my view they have a duty to fix unsafe health information, and these are the questions where published studies have found AI falling short. The way to reach them is the public scorecards they already compete on, called benchmarks, so the second audience is the people who run those scorecards, such as GlobalDentBench and Kaggle Benchmarks: we ask them to add the questions. Clinicians and scientists write and check the questions, so each right answer comes from published evidence, not from us. And the people in this room, funders, health leaders and policy makers, are not the end users: they are who can make it happen, by backing a 180-day pilot and making introductions. The impact is one question at a time. Someone on a bone-strengthening medicine asks if a tooth can be pulled. The unsafe answer says yes, it is routine. The answer we want asks about the medicine first and says to tell the dentist before any extraction, because in some patients on these medicines the jawbone does not heal after an extraction, and that is permanent. The next slides show why this matters and what it would take. If asked how we would know it worked: the same questions, re-run on the same models after the results are published, show whether unsafe answers went down; that is the measure, and nothing has been measured yet. If asked whether the AI companies will listen: it is their decision; public scores are what they compete on, which is why the questions go on the scorecard.
