The pattern in medicine: more than 80% of AI's severe errors were things it left out, not things it got wrong.

The example illustrates a harm GlobalDentBench (2026) names. The 80% is from NOHARM (Stanford-led ARISE network, 2026 preprint, version 4) · 4

Here is what unsafe means. The study found AI is most dangerous in two areas. Where medicines and medical conditions are involved, about one in seven answers risked harm that cannot be undone. On anesthesia and medical emergencies, about one in eleven. The simplest example: someone on a bone-strengthening medicine for osteoporosis asks if it is fine to have a tooth pulled. The unsafe answer says yes, it is routine, and never asks about the medicine. In some patients on these medicines the jawbone does not heal after an extraction, and that is permanent. If asked for detail: the figures are 14.15 percent for the category the study calls Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety, and 8.93 percent for Anesthesia and Medical Emergencies, averaged across 12 AI models. The study lists this jaw condition, medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, among its examples of irreversible harm, along with avoidable tooth loss, permanent nerve injury and delayed treatment of cancer or severe infection. The question and answer on this slide are my illustration of that harm: the study publishes the rates, not the individual wrong answers. The risk to any one patient is low at osteoporosis doses and higher at cancer-treatment doses, which is why the dentist needs to know about the medicine. The line at the bottom is from a different study, called First, Do NOHARM, from the Stanford-led ARISE network. It tested 20 large language models and 4 clinical AI tools on 1,100 tasks: 100 cases drawn from real doctor-to-specialist consultations, plus 10 variants of each, chosen by specialists, that change details without changing the right advice. Omissions, things the AI left out, account for more than 80 percent of the severe errors. That is the same pattern as this example, and it is also how AI fails on social factors: an answer that never asks about cost, coverage or transportation leaves out what decides whether the person gets care. If asked for detail: that is the paper's current version, version 4 of July 2026, and it is about severe errors, not every harmful error. Its first version, of December 2025, tested 31 models on the 100 cases: omissions were 76.6 percent of the severely harmful errors and 50.2 percent, about half, of all harmful errors. NOHARM is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed, and it has no oral health cases. If asked for an emergency example: a person with a swollen face and a fever from a toothache is told to rinse with salt water and wait; a spreading infection can become life-threatening.
