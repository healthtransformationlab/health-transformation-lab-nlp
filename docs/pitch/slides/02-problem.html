First, how AI gets graded. Independent researchers publish tests and score every major AI on them, like a public report card. The technical word is benchmark. AI companies compete to raise those scores, so what is on the scorecard is what gets better. Now four facts from the first dental scorecard. One: AI scores 81 percent on multiple-choice questions from dental licensing exams. Two: on questions built from published patient case reports, the score falls to 22 percent. Three: nearly one in three of those answers was rated unsafe, and about one in twenty risked harm that cannot be undone. Four, and this is the one we can change: we found nothing that tests the social side. No scorecard we found checks whether AI answers accurately on the social factors that decide whether people get care: cost, coverage, distance and transportation. US emergency rooms see about 1.9 million visits a year for tooth problems, and Medicaid is the most common payer for them (CDC, NCHS Data Brief 531, 2025); for people without a dentist, the untested questions are the ones that matter most. Why now: the first global dental scorecard for AI was published this year, so the questions are being written right now, and the people who show up shape them. If asked for detail: the first three are from GlobalDentBench, published in 2026: 12 leading AI models, from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, xAI and others, 8,978 questions from 88 countries; the scores are averages across all 12, not one company's product, and Google's model scored highest overall; 31 percent of case answers were unsafe and 4.5 percent carried a risk of irreversible harm. The fourth is from a first search of benchmarks, not a full review: existing tests check whether AI can spot social factors in medical records, or whether answers are biased, but none we found checks accuracy on access to care. If asked whether anyone has studied AI in dentistry: yes. A systematic review of 38 studies (Kaplan and Akyol, Japanese Dental Science Review, 2026; literature searched January 2025) found no universally accepted framework for evaluating AI in dentistry, test methods and sizes that vary widely, mostly simulated scenarios rather than real-world use, and limited evidence on how patients interpret and act on AI answers. Its categories are patient questions about treatment, diagnosis, model comparisons and decision support; none is access to care. The authors also say continuous monitoring is essential because new models keep appearing.
